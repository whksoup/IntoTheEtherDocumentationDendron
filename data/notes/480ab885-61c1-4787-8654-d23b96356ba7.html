Zeptobars — decapsulated die-shot archive

The problem

Most people who could benefit from seeing inside a chip — students, reverse engineers, people trying to spot counterfeits — never will, because decapsulation is destructive, requires hazardous acid, and the resulting images mostly stay in private engineering reports. There was no ongoing, public, growing archive of what commodity silicon actually looks like underneath its package.

What they did

Zeptobars have run a "weekend die-shot" practice for close to two decades: strip a chip's plastic package with acid, photograph the exposed die under a microscope, and publish the image with a short technical note — die dimensions in micrometres, sometimes a schematic if one is known, sometimes a note on provenance or a counterfeit found along the way. The output reads as a running catalogue rather than a single project: one entry at a time, same format, accumulating into a public reference of what different generations and categories of chip look like inside. They've stated openly that the goal includes education (getting engineers curious about physical implementation, not just HDL) and detection (spotting remarked or non-genuine parts), alongside the pure documentary value.

What we take

The cadence is the transferable part more than the photography itself: a small, repeatable unit of work (one chip, one post, one fixed format) sustained over years outperforms an ambitious one-off project. That maps onto our own "entries" section under notes.photos — better to add one source at a time in the fixed three-heading shape than to wait for a comprehensive survey. The image side is more straightforward macro/micro photography than IRIS's approach, so it's a useful contrast entry to file alongside it: destructive but simple and cheap versus non-destructive but more technically involved.

What doesn't transfer: decapsulation is destructive and uses concentrated acid, which is neither safe nor appropriate for objects we can't sacrifice — most of what we're photographing for the exhibition has to survive being photographed.
