
## The problem

A chip is a black box by default: once it's soldered into a product, there's
no ordinary way to confirm it contains what its label says it contains, or
that nothing extra has been added to it in the supply chain. The
conventional way to look — decapsulation — means desoldering the part and
boiling it in acid, which destroys the very unit you wanted to verify and
tells you nothing about the units still in the field. Huang's framing is
explicit: cryptography can build a chain of trust rooted in a secure
element, but nothing tells you whether to trust the secure element itself.
Any inspection method that actually helps has to work in place, cheaply,
after the chip has already shipped.

## What they did

IRIS (Infra-Red, In-Situ) exploits the fact that silicon is transparent to
infrared light above roughly 1000 nm. Shining a strong IR source through the
backside of a still-packaged, still-soldered chip and picking up the
reflection with a lightly modified, off-the-shelf digital camera reveals the
metal interconnect layers underneath — enough to make out block-level
features like RAM, core count, and peripherals. Later refinements addressed
the angle of the light itself: because the incidence angle changes which
metal features are visible, Huang built a two-axis rig that swings a pair of
IR sources through an arc centered on the microscope's focal point, so the
zenith and azimuth of the illumination can both be adjusted independently.
The full method, including hardware plans, is published openly rather than
run as a paid inspection service.

## What we take

This is the strongest match in the whole reference set to our actual claim
— that a technology whose case rests on being inspectable has to be
photographed as if inspection were the point. IRIS goes further than most
of the seams we've collected: it's non-destructive and repeatable on the
exact unit a viewer might hold, not a sacrificed twin. The variable-angle
lighting rig is the most transferable single idea — a fixed capture
condition isn't always a single fixed light, it can be a *controlled range*
of lighting used consistently across subjects, documented like an
instrument setting rather than an artistic choice. Also worth taking:
Huang's own comparison of IRIS to signature verification for software — the
photograph isn't just documentation, it's the trust mechanism itself, which
is close to what we want the exhibition wall to argue.

What doesn't transfer: IRIS depends on properties specific to silicon and
IR wavelengths; it's a technique for photographing chips, not a general
lighting convention we can apply to enclosures, racks, or installed
hardware.
