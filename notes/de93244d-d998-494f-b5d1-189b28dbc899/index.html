Conservation photography — raking light

The problem

Flat, even lighting — our default rule for the whole exhibition — is exactly wrong for one category of subject: anything where the information lives in surface relief rather than surface pattern. A scratched enclosure, a warped panel, a board with visible rework or damage reads as featureless under flat light, because flat light is specifically designed to suppress the shadows that would otherwise reveal that relief. Conservators face this constantly when documenting paintings, prints, and objects for condition records.

What they did

Raking light photography places a single light source at a very shallow angle — roughly 5 to 10 degrees — nearly parallel to the object's surface, usually in an otherwise darkened space. Because the light grazes rather than floods the surface, any texture or relief throws a shadow: brushwork, tool marks, warping, cracking, prior repairs. Conservators typically shoot several raking-light exposures per object, each with the light coming from a different direction, because a single angle can hide relief that runs parallel to it. It's used specifically for condition recording and technical examination, not for the primary "what does this look like" documentary shot, which is usually made separately under flat light.

What we take

This belongs as a deliberate exception to our flat-lighting rule rather than a replacement for it: use flat light for the primary declared-scale documentary shot of every object, and add a raking-light pass only for subjects where surface condition or construction detail is the actual point — worn enclosures, hand-modified boards, installed hardware showing wear. Shooting from more than one raking angle, the way conservators do, is worth adopting directly if we go this route, since a single angle can miss exactly the detail we wanted it for.

What doesn't transfer: raking light works on subjects that are close to flat and hold still under a fixed rig; it's not a general substitute for our flat-lit convention, and applying it everywhere would reintroduce the mystique and drama our four rules explicitly rule out.
