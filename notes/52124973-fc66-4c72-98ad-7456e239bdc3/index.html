iFixit teardown photography

The problem

A teardown has to work as an instruction, not an illustration. Someone with the device open on their own bench needs to match what they're seeing to what they're reading, under time pressure, with one hand occupied. That rules out the usual product-photography moves: the flattering angle, the shallow depth of field, the dramatic single light. It also has to be produced at volume by different people across years and still look like one system, or the reader loses the thread between guides.

What they did

A fixed overhead camera over a plain seamless background, flat even lighting, minimal shadow, everything shot square to the sensor. The frame stays put; the object changes. Steps are numbered and each one shows the state of the device at that step, so the sequence itself carries the information — the reader is comparing consecutive frames, not decoding a single hero image.

On top of that sits an annotation layer: coloured circles, arrows and outlines drawn over the photograph to mark the screw, the clip, the ribbon cable. The photograph is deliberately plain so the annotation has somewhere to live. Tools and hands appear in frame when the action matters, not for warmth.

The colour-coding is doing more work than the photography here. Worth pulling the annotation convention out into its own note once we've got a second example of it.

What we take

Directly transferable: the fixed rig and the discipline of not moving it. Our declared-capture-conditions rule is essentially this, and iFixit proves it scales across contributors, which is exactly our risk — an exhibition shot by several people over months.

Also transferable: the annotation layer as a first-class element rather than a caption. For open silicon this is the whole argument. A board photographed plainly and then annotated is inspectable; the same board lit dramatically is a trophy.

What doesn't transfer: the overhead-only grammar assumes a roughly flat object on a bench. Racks, enclosures and installed field hardware need a second convention, and the exhibition has more of those than it has boards. The sequence logic also assumes disassembly — a linear process with a clear next state. Most of our subjects aren't being taken apart, so we'd be borrowing the frame discipline without the narrative that justifies it.

Open question for the wall layout: does the step-sequence idea survive at all if there's no disassembly? Maybe the sequence becomes fabrication stages rather than steps. Needs a trial before we commit the grid.
